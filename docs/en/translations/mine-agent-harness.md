# There Are Many Agent Harnesses, but This One Is Mine

*A non-engineer's note*

I am not an engineer. At a company that builds Agent Harness software for programmers typing in black terminal windows, I used to feel I had to say this quietly. Everyone around me was shipping code, while I wrote documentation, made plans, and talked to people. That work was certainly useful, but I was not really using what we made.

So for a while I did what many people quietly do around AI: I nodded along. I had heard “Agent”, “loop”, “Harness”, and “Extension” often enough that I could nod quite convincingly in meetings. When others treated TUI as common knowledge, I quietly looked up what it was; by the time I finally understood, people had moved on to talking about APIs, and I was back at the start. Borrowing a line from our own “[What is an Agent Harness?](/en/translations/what-is-a-harness)”, I was the person who “did not ask because I was embarrassed”.

Later, I stopped just nodding and did something better: I opened Pi and started asking questions.

The Harness does not care that you cannot program. Just open it, write down what you want in natural language, and it starts working. I thought I would surely hit a dead end, stuck because I lacked knowledge. In reality, I only had to keep writing down what I needed. The first time I asked it to read a long, ugly PDF and then tell me the three things I really needed to know, it simply did it. I felt a small surge of power that was even a little ridiculous.

Behind its intimidating appearance, the Harness is just a tool: it turns “I wish something could do this for me” into “done”.

## Making it mine

The Pi use I love most has nothing at all to do with software development. Every morning it reads my inbox and meeting notes, finds the things I actually promised other people, and produces a tidy task list: one group for what I owe others, another for what I am waiting on.

I did not hire anyone, and I did not learn to program first. I simply described this job once, and it turned into a small workflow running on my computer, shaped entirely around how I think.

When curiosity struck and I wanted to open the hood and look inside, Pi also proved a very patient teacher. Once I asked it to guide me through writing a Google Apps Script. It walked me through the whole process step by step, like asking the most generous software engineer colleague to explain: using plain language, and never making you feel stupid.

In the middle of one explanation, code stopped being so intimidating. Now I build things myself too. The results are not spectacular; most of them are small tools that genuinely make work and life lighter: making personalized digital invitations, managing email engagement metrics, and automatically tidying data with a spreadsheet.

That is when the slogan finally meant something to me: there are many agent harnesses, but this one is yours.

I used to read that line as a message for engineers — for those who open the hood and change the wiring. Now I understand “yours” differently. It does not mean you have to build the tool yourself, but that whoever you are, you can adapt it to your life.

I made my Pi revolve around a messy inbox. Others might make theirs revolve around lesson planning, research, or small-business bookkeeping.

## Why this matters more than my task list

Pi is free, open source, and runs on my own laptop. That used to sound like a mere technical footnote. Now I understand why it matters: I can shape it to fit how I work.

What really made the difference for me is that Pi can read its own code, explain how it works, and then help me change it. It is as if the tool can teach me how to rebuild the tool itself. When the original text was written, Pi users had shared more than 5,000 Extensions with one another, freely handing over workflows they had built to other people.

Much discussion about AI centers on what this technology will do to us. From where I stand, the truly exciting change is this: I no longer have to wait for someone technical to build me the tool I want.

I am still not an engineer, but I no longer say it quietly. It turns out that owning your own tool does not require you to become an engineer first; you only need enough curiosity to ask, and enough courage to keep going.

——Jakob, Earendil Founders Associate
